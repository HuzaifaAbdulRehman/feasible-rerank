Penalty Barriers and Fairness Budgets

Selecting a recommendation list as one QUBO over relevance, diversity and group exposure. Two results: the textbook encoding fails silently, and once fixed, the method's real advantage is not accuracy but feasibility — it reaches fairness targets no classical baseline here can reach at all.

Findings

Three results, in the order they were found

01

The standard QUBO recipe is broken, and it fails silently

Encoding “exactly k” as a penalty and handing the problem to a simulated annealer produces lists that are always the right length and close to arbitrary among lists of that length. Nothing looks wrong: the constraint is satisfied 100% of the time. But the annealer loses to a hand-written greedy heuristic on its own objective.

Across 5 seeds, neal SA 0.475 ± 0.030 against fixed solvers 0.692 ± 0.038 NDCG — a gap of more than five standard deviations.

02

The operating point decides the comparison — by more than the methods differ

At λ=4, μ=0 every QUBO variant loses to group-quota MMR on essentially everything. At λ=0, μ=1 it does not. The first configuration switches the fairness term off and then scores the result on fairness, which pits a QUBO with no reason to optimise exposure against a baseline with quotas hard-coded.

03

The advantage is feasibility, not accuracy

Tune every method on one half of the users, score it on the other. At a fairness requirement of τ ≤ 0.25, no classical baseline can satisfy the constraint at any setting of its own hyperparameters. The QUBO can, and still returns NDCG 0.904. Loosen to τ ≥ 0.30 and quota-MMR becomes feasible and the accuracy gap closes.

A paired test over 200 users settles what “closes” means: the residual gap is real (p ≈ 2×10−7) and its median size is 0.0012 NDCG. The parity gap, by contrast, is −0.10 and goes the QUBO's way on 115 users and against it on zero.

The headline result

Accuracy available at each fairness requirement

τ is a declared fairness budget — an upper bound on mean exposure-parity deviation, the way a deployer would actually state the requirement. Every method, baselines included, is tuned to maximise NDCG@10 subject to that budget on a tuning half of the users, then scored once on the disjoint other half.

τ = 0.22 – 0.25

Tight

Only the QUBO methods meet the budget at all. Quota-MMR bottoms out at 0.2517 ± 0.0067 parity — a quota mechanism can only redistribute slots it has. The QUBO penalty targets parity directly and reaches 0.1983, essentially the arithmetic floor.

τ = 0.30 – 0.40

Moderate

Quota-MMR becomes feasible and the accuracy gap closes to nothing: 0.9033 ± 0.0115 against 0.9043 ± 0.0109. The QUBO still holds a non-overlapping parity advantage, so it buys fairness headroom rather than accuracy — at roughly 100× the compute.

τ = 1.00

Unconstrained

Greedy top-k wins by definition. Both QUBO solvers select λ=0, μ=0 and return NDCG 1.0000, recovering greedy exactly. Told that fairness does not matter, the tuner concludes the QUBO should not be used — the protocol's built-in correctness check.

Retracted

An earlier version of this analysis claimed the QUBO beat quota-MMR on NDCG as well. That margin was measured with λ and μ selected on the same data they were scored on, while the baseline sat at its default. Under the disjoint split it disappears: 0.9043 ± 0.0109 against 0.9033 ± 0.0115, a gap of 0.001 against a spread of 0.011. The parity advantage survived; the accuracy claim did not.

Finding 01, in detail

Why the penalty encoding defeats the annealer

The failure is structural, not a matter of tuning. Two valid k-item lists are never adjacent under single-bit flips: moving between them means removing one item and adding another, and the state in between has k±1 items and pays the full penalty P.

So the sampler faces a dilemma with no good side. Cold enough to resolve the objective and it cannot cross the barrier, freezing in whichever valid list it first stumbled into. Hot enough to cross and the objective is thermal noise. On this benchmark the cardinality term's largest coefficient is 737.8 against the objective's 1.0.

Four independent lines of evidence pin it down: the annealer's energy is worse than greedy MMR's on the same problem; raising the sweep count tenfold makes it slightly worse; lowering the penalty strength does not help, because the barrier height is the strength; and tabu search — the same encoding, the same move set, but with memory instead of temperature — solves it.

Why this matters beyond one repo

A physical quantum annealer receives the same penalty-encoded problem and the same barrier. A near-random valid list still scores well on diversity and coverage, so a paper reporting only downstream metrics would show a clean result and never notice the sampler had optimised nothing.

Statistics

Significant and negligible are not opposites

Comparing means over 3 seeds is a test with n=3. Every method sees the identical candidate set for the identical user, so their per-user scores are paired — which makes the same data a test with n=200. The seeds were never the sample.

The NDCG row is why this test was worth running. The seed-level comparison called it a tie. The paired test says the difference is certain — and that its median size is 0.0012. Both readings are correct, and either one alone misleads: a table of p-values would call this a win, a table of means called it a tie. It is a difference we can be confident exists and that is far too small to matter.

The parity row has the opposite shape. A median improvement of 0.10, better on 115 users and worse on none. That is not a marginal edge, and it is the same effect the fairness-budget curve shows from the other direction.

Recall does not move at all — 194 of 200 users tie. Under leave-one-out there is at most one relevant item per user against a candidate-set ceiling of 0.49, so most users score identically under every reranker. A recall difference on this benchmark would be noise.

Read the interval, not the star

With 200 users a p-value of 10−25 reports consistency, not magnitude. It is driven by the QUBO improving parity for every single user whose list changes at all, and says nothing by itself about whether 0.10 is a lot. That is why every row carries an effect size and a count.

Method

How the comparison is kept honest

Two decisions do most of the work, and both were added after an earlier version of this analysis got the answer wrong.

The baselines are tuned too. MMR and quota-MMR have a trade-off parameter of their own. Searching the QUBO's two weights while leaving the baseline's at its default is the same asymmetry that produced the wrong conclusion the first time, pointed the other way. Quota-MMR genuinely uses its search here — it picks a more diverse setting under tight budgets and a more accurate one under loose budgets.

Selection is constrained. Maximising NDCG alone always returns λ=0, μ=0, which is greedy top-k: perfect accuracy by construction and the worst fairness available. A selection rule on a multi-objective problem has to state what it trades, and this one states it in the units the requirement is written in.

Limitations

What is still weak

Stated here rather than left for a reader to find.

Small evaluation sample

The budget curve uses three seeds and 40 evaluation users. Visible in the τ=0.20 column, where the QUBO's parity of 0.1983 ± 0.0017 straddles the budget and is marked infeasible on the seeds where tuning landed just above. The paired tests above use 200 users and are far better powered, but they run at a fixed operating point rather than inside the split protocol; combining the two is the remaining piece.

One solver's timing is a stopwatch, not a measurement

The tabu sampler stops on a 20 ms wall-clock timeout, so its search depth — and therefore its quality — depends on the machine. Running the same seeds on battery and on mains changed its NDCG from 0.8801 to 0.8873 while the fixed-work solvers were identical to four decimals. The headline result is this solver's, so this qualifies it directly.

Groups are popularity tiers, not categories

The ratings export carries no product metadata, so items are cut into four equal-sized popularity tiers — the standard short-head/long-tail partition. This makes the fairness term fight the exact bias the retrieval model exhibits, but it is not the same as seller or category fairness.

One dataset

One catalogue with one similarity structure cannot say whether the operating-point effect generalises. Two more datasets are the next item after the paired tests.
